# Juniors or Seniors, Who Will Stand?

Who will stand — the Junior who has everything to prove, or the Senior who has everything to lose? Both of them stared at the same AI revolution and felt the same cold wind. The answer, as promised, might surprise you... Spoiler: neither of them will stand because of their code.

## The Junior

I remember my first job as a junior. I was still studying in the University, probably in 8th semester, when one of my teachers recruited me for his startup company. My first assignment was a project for creating stored procedures about employees' payroll in Informix using UNIX for a large grocery store client, and I had zero idea of how to use any of those technologies, and I had even less knowledge about payrolls (it was my first job remember?). I felt lost, overwhelmed and the most stupid guy in the room when I was the cockiest, proudest student in the class because of my *outstanding* grades.

But even with my ego shattered on the floor, I was happy. I was learning an insane amount of information, I was thrilled for every new discovery, I was getting paid, and I could say *Sorry, I got to go to work ;)*.

And I don't blame the University, they prepared me for other things like structured thinking with dozens of math courses. And in a world with hundreds of technologies in every possible software domain, it becomes impossible to prepare a person for any combination of them.

**The University gives you the confidence the real world will destroy in the first week of working**

After some months I got assigned to another project, a Knowledge Transfer from a company which was leaving a custom made program for a clothing manufacturing factory. This time it was PowerBuilder along with SQL Server. I was familiar with PowerBuilder from the University, and I loved it! SQL Server had SQL (lol) and I knew some of it already because of the Informix project, but I still had to learn the visual environment Informix didn't have. 

So, here we are again, starting from zero in an unknown territory. Less lost, I got some sort of experience already, still asking silly junior questions and breaking stuff now and then (I'll never forget my first **DELETE FROM TABLE**, and I say "first" because I did a few).

And that's the thing I love of working in software companies, every few months a new project arrives: new faces, new industry, new use cases, new learnings. 

**Feeling a little lost, but less, every time.**

We normally hire juniors for simple, repetitive, boring tasks like building unit tests to reduce the technical debt. Or to be on-call for supporting an application. Or for documenting purposes. None of those tasks will break the system while they learn and build up confidence, in themselves and with the team.

But AI is good for repetitive tasks, right? However, the AI adoption is not equal across all companies, and even less across projects in the same company. And not only because they can't homologate AI usage inside the company with AI governance and guidelines, but because of the projects themselves: some solutions are still monoliths, some are single-repo with multiple microservices while others have a repo per microservice. This makes each team dynamics, and necessities different.

**Even if AI does the repetitive stuff, someone has to point it in the right direction and trigger it.**


Now, there are two rules for hiring a junior:

1. He must be good at something: it could be in backend, or frontend, or database. Preferably in all of the above, but good for the project's most basic needs.

2. He must be cheap: an expensive junior will eat the project's margin.

And I'd like to add a new one:

3. **He must be good using AI**

### Can a Junior get a job nowadays?

In reality, getting a job is difficult for everybody regardless of the seniority level. Every project tries to find the candidate that makes a perfect fit and with so many technologies the combinations are endless. And now with AI, repetitive tasks seem to be already taken.

Most companies or projects don't have patience, budget or sufficient repetitive tasks backlog... or that's what they think. As I said before, *The lack of specs will become the technical debt of the future* and I am no saying by any means that a junior will be responsible of creating new specs or critical ones. But there are some specs that are indeed simple and repetitive like CRUD specs that don't exist in brownfield projects, and given the spec debt there could be dozens of missing specs in a single project. Sounds like a job for a junior, right?

The junior must add value to a team instead of babysitting him, as any member of a team he is an investment and the investment must be fruitful. So, how to prepare? How to earn the opportunity to demonstrate you can add value to a team of seniors?

The opportunities exist but now companies expect more from the people now that AI facilitates learning. 

**It's expected that you learn faster and become a Pi-shaped engineer straight out of school and not as a result of a long mentoring process.**

I know it's tough, but not impossible. For instance, in the company I'm currently working, we help them to achieve Frontend + Backend + AI skills by promoting learning courses oriented to achieve this level.

**The bar has been raised and the typical shy, quiet, single-programming-language Junior is not enough anymore.**

**Do not spend too much time in learning *how to code*, but instead in *how to tell the machine what to code***


### Lessons for the Junior Job-Seeker
Here are some recommendations to my junior self, things that I would change if I'd start over:

1. **Don't waste time**, every day without learning something new is a wasted day. 

2. **Invest time in networking with others**, late in the career I understood that relationships took me further than grade notes.

3. **Stay humble, stay hungry**, no matter how good you are there will be always someone better (or something like AI). So keep learning and keep sharing your knowledge with others.

4. **Don't stay put**, the technology ocean is vast and deep, learn how to sail those waters. Don't stay too long on the shore, don't dive too deep. 



## The Senior

Recently I re-encountered a University colleague after two decades. She joined the largest utilities company in the city when she left the University, that was the dream job back then because of the benefits and stability. She told me she had felt stuck for years already, the politic game to get promoted was not fair and no matter what technology you master, you cannot decide, you cannot experiment, you cannot feel the overwhelming sensation of being lost ever again.

She became valuable but only for that company. Getting a job in an AI-first company is not possible, and a ramp-down would be catastrophic for her.

Another friend of mine, this time from a previous job. He is a master in SQL Server, he can tune up any query no matter how extensive or complex it is. He knows his craft, he knows all the tricks needed to get the job done. However, he got stuck in that niche. SQL Server is not a preferred database for the microservices architectural style, NoSQL databases or less heavyweight SQL databases like PostgreSQL rule in this area due to less enterprise licensing and the monolithic mindset that comes with them. And this is a significant problem, the Monolith architecture is not a trend but a legacy.

**It is not that he is technically bad, but architecturally unfashioned**

Let me be clear, this is not the job's or company's fault. Or *The world changed too fast!*. No, we all make decisions, we all see the technological trends, the job offers asking for technologies we haven't even heard of, we all watch the TED Talks or ignore to watch them. 

And I was one of them! (sort of). One of my previous jobs was in the Public Safety industry, building solutions for 9-1-1 systems. The use cases were incredibly complex and awesome, visiting those places with the law enforcement was a dream. But I was focused in the company's problems and I didn't realized it until I got fired due to "strategic layoffs" and faced the reality of being old dated in a constantly changing industry. I thought I could settle down because I felt irreplaceable, I was the top architect leading the company's innovation. I ignored my surroundings.

And this is something I tell my talents every day:

**Even working on a modern project makes you outdated**

Why? Because projects need time to be built, get released and mature. This can take years depending on the size of the project. And during that time new technologies emerge.

**Your professional career is a consequence of personal decisions made during years of work**

### Are Seniors going to be replaced with AI?

We all have watched the news about *AI layoffs*. Not all of them are clear about how many Seniors and what they were doing inside those companies. But in most cases those layoffs are needed for companies with high payrolls and low margins, and we know Seniors are expensive.

But leaving that aside, and keeping in mind that many companies regret those layoffs, a good balanced Senior (cost vs value outcomes) is more valuable than an empty AI. The costs of AI cannot be overlooked, they can be cheap at first glance but when a damage is done the cost can be massive not just in money but reputational.

**What is expected for a Senior is the ability to adapt and incorporate AI to increase productivity**

Seniors know better than anyone what the user wants, how the company works, what are the pain points and what can be improved. Losing that knowledge is a suicide. 

**The flexibility and ability to create Proof of Concepts that bring value to the company is key for your stability.**

Imagine a company with 1000 employees facing three options: 

- Option A: layoff 500 employees.
- Option B: keep the 1000 and augment them with AI.
- Option C: do nothing and wait to see what happens. 

For simplicity, let's assume that AI can replace a person entirely or augment his production in 100%, then:

- Option A reduces costs but keeps the same productivity.
- Option B increases the cost but doubles the output volume increasing the revenue. 
- Option C stayed the same, no more costs and no more productivity.

As you can see, by keeping *and augmenting the people with AI* is a better strategy than reducing them or not doing anything at all.

**New teams are expected to be smaller, but more productive.**

With AI, these years of learning and professional evolution are undervalued, it programs faster and better. In a world where anyone can speak their own native language, then anyone should be able to create any program. So, if that were true, then the Software Engineering career wouldn't be necessary either, correct? Only for those who'd maintain the machines running in cold datacenters.

But speaking English doesn't make you a great book writer, or a philosopher or an engineer. The natural language is what we use to communicate with others but words don't build the world, actions do. An LLM model will create an application for sure, and it will test it, and even deploy it, but as easy as it sounds is as easy as it will fail because we have built dozens of controls and steps to make sure the final user, and the business, never gets disrupted or frustrated.

Non-technical users don't know these controls by heart: feature flags, blue/green deployments, canary deployments, feature branches, PRs, linters, among others. And even if AI assistants include them, that doesn't relieve you of the responsibility.

**It’s like the self-parking feature in modern cars. Just because they can park themselves doesn't mean you shouldn’t learn how to do it yourself.**

And this also applies not just for programming techniques but also for elicitation techniques. Understanding the customer is the most critical task in the success or failure of a project. Many developers hide behind the Team Lead and leave the responsibility of asking questions to him instead of becoming a protagonist.

**Being an M-Shaped Senior engineer doesn't save your career by itself. It makes you difficult to replace, but not impossible.**


## The Balance Sheet

### AI brings challenges for both Juniors and Seniors

For a Junior, telling the AI what to do seems simple but if they haven't mastered the Design Patterns, Best Practices and other building blocks the resulting code will be functional but hard to maintain, insecure, hard to scale, hard to extend. Most engineers, especially Juniors, are not familiar with Non-Functional Requirements or architectural styles. And building a program with just *user intents* is not enough.

For a Senior, leaving the control to an AI agent feels awkward and irresponsible. It's like leaving his well-maintained program to a recently joined team member. Even if you let the AI explore your code, you still don't trust it for big changes. Documenting specs seems less productive than just building the feature as usual.

For instance, I have used AI Personas myself in green-field projects and these agents are great companions, similar to pair programming. The architecture role is a lonely one because normally there's only one architect per team or group of teams, and having an AI Architect Persona who knows your codebase from top to bottom, with whom you can share your opinions and construct ideas by guiding it, feels just right. It cannot replace my judgement, it cannot see everything I see or sense what I feel about my team or work environment but it shines light where I'm not looking. 

AI can only learn from its training and from the project's codebase, but not from other projects in the company. Seniors have accumulated knowledge in multiple projects in the company and from the project's history, this is not easy to dump into a spec file. 

Personally, I have seen projects with 166 microservices connected to a single database with 328 tables. That can only be explained by a senior who has left his own skin in the game. Even if you ask AI to explore all the codebase and all the tables, it won't be able to explain *why* the system ended up like this.

**"An AI is a senior in every language and a junior in every codebase"**


### Pros and Cons of Juniors

| # | ✅ Pros | ❌ Cons |
|---|---------|---------|
| 1 | **Cost-effective** — lower salary preserves project margin | **Narrow depth** — skills are superficial until trust is built and responsibility grows |
| 2 | **High energy & curiosity** — thrilled by every new discovery, fast adopters of new tools | **Needs mentoring** — requires a senior's time and patience; babysitting has a cost ("long story, ask John") |
| 3 | **Good fit for repetitive tasks** — unit tests, on-call support, documentation, CRUD specs | **Can break things** — limited experience with production controls (feature flags, canary deployments, PRs, linters) |
| 4 | **Reduces technical debt backlog** — ideal for filling spec gaps that seniors deprioritize | **Slow to build trust** — higher-responsibility tasks must wait until the team gains confidence in them |
| 5 | **AI amplified** — proctored learning is no longer an obstacle; juniors can learn and deliver faster than ever | **Requires clear direction** — even when AI does the repetitive work, someone still needs to point it in the right direction |
| 6 | **No fear of looking dumb** — juniors ask the obvious questions that seniors stopped asking years ago | **Low confidence under pressure** — when something breaks in production, the junior freezes; they lack the scar tissue to act decisively |


### Pros and Cons of Seniors

| # | ✅ Pros | ❌ Cons |
|---|---------|---------|
| 1 | **Battle-tested depth** — knows the production controls that keep systems stable: feature flags, canary deployments, blue/green, PRs, linters, rollback strategies | **Higher cost** — seniority commands a premium salary that weighs on project margins if not well-utilized |
| 2 | **Architectural thinking** — can evaluate trade-offs across the whole system, not just the ticket in front of them | **Risk of I-Shape** — years of deep specialization can quietly erode breadth; a master of yesterday's stack may be architecturally unfashioned today |
| 3 | **Multiplier effect** — a single senior guiding a team of juniors delivers more value than the same hours spent coding alone | **Resistance to change** — accumulated habits and opinions can slow adoption of new tools, paradigms, or AI-assisted workflows |
| 4 | **Institutional memory** — carries the *why* behind decisions, preventing the team from repeating expensive mistakes | **Settling down** — the comfort of expertise is also its trap; without deliberate exposure to new challenges, growth stalls |
| 5 | **AI leverage** — can direct AI tools with precision, validate output critically, and catch the subtle failures a junior (or a business user) would miss | **Complacency with learning** — seniority can breed the illusion that there is nothing left to learn, exactly when the industry demands the opposite |
| 6 | **Calibrated confidence** — prudent and deliberate by default, but when the situation demands action they know exactly what lever to pull and why | **Overcaution as a blocker** — that same prudence can tip into paralysis or gatekeeping, slowing a team that needs to move fast |


Seems like neither of them has earned a safe place in the AI era. But we have to look a little bit closer, not in the technical spectrum but in the human...


## The Human Skills

We talked about the different shapes, but all of them miss the most important area nowadays with AI: the **Soft/Human Skills**. This is the foundation to every person Junior or Senior. 

When I got my Java Enterprise Architect certification I wasn't even close to being a real architect. I did it mostly because of my competitive spirit but I lacked of all the necessary skills to really understand the client and to lead a team of people. I had little charisma, little *Team Collaboration* (I thought of myself as a genius in his ivory tower who will never have to code ever again), I was really good with my *Presentation Skills* but without *Business Communication* and *Storytelling* that was pointless. And I really struggled with *Openness to Change* especially as a Senior. 

My *Emotional Intelligence* was zero, an absolute zero. And I thank God for pointing me to my therapist who saved me. My anxiety blocked me from achieving a solid *Stress Management*, I remember going to Emergency with stomach pain every time I came from business trip for a production release in the most stressful project I've ever been. The anxiety was also a problem for *Decision Making*. Can you imagine an architect struggling to make a decision?

I was not a lost cause, I had great sense of *Negotiation*, *Complex Problem Solving*, *Prioritization*, *Influencing Others*, *Leadership* (even with my anxiety) and *Learning Agility*. I always knew in my heart that I can learn anything.

With time people, and myself, started noticing my improvements, it came with better performance evaluations from the customer. I was able to stand tough interviews with architects and customers with ease, moving from a Software Architect to a Solutions Architect responsible of more applications and larger teams.

One day, my leaders trusted me with larger responsibilities beyond of the architect's common assignments by promoting me to an extra-mile role of Technical Manager of 300 people in a company. And I say extra-mile because I still had my regular architectural assignment. I could have said "no" and continued with my standard role for which I signed, but my incipient *Mentoring* and *Coaching* skills called me. I have to admit I had difficult meetings, especially when discussing salaries and dismissals but that improved my *Conflict Resolution*.

**Every new experience will take you to another level eventually.**

When I changed jobs I restarted as a Solution Architect, but my human and technical skills bubbled up again to leadership. I was assigned as a Talent Manager of 60 and one year later I got promoted to Head of Core Engineering leading ~700. I could mention more soft skills but you got the point already:

**Without human skills, anyone could be replaced by AI, even the M-Shaped.**

Even though AI can do some of the above like Knowledge Sharing more patiently than a human teacher could do, the human has not evolved yet to trust AI blindly. Business deals are made between humans; negotiations, leadership and ownership can't simply be emulated. Nevertheless, they can be augmented or taught with the help of AI.

Take me as an example, I've never written a book before and it took me multiple loops with AI to improve because my storytelling skill was not fully trained. But here I am, doing what I was not capable before improving page by page with the help of AI. It's not AI who is writing, it's me.

I know Seniors with little ability on social skills because they preferred to train their technical skills instead, they decided to hide behind the Team Lead and let the other do the talking. And now with AI those technical skills are easily replaced, but building soft skills takes years, years of human interactions, years of interacting with other humans to become a better human.

And I think it's key to understand where each person fits, as I said before every project is different and it has specific needs. As an Engineer Manager, it is critical to know the right combination between seniority and new blood. Advanced projects using AI need the seniority for the right judgement, while legacy projects will still need seniors for their knowledge but also juniors for the succession plan while incorporating AI for productivity in some areas.
