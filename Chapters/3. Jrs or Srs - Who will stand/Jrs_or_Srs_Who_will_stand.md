## Juniors or Seniors, Who Will Stand?

### The Junior

I remember my first job as a junior. I was still studying in the University, probably in 8th semester, when one of my teachers recruited my for his start up company. My first assignment was a project for creating stored procedures about employee's payroll in Informix using UNIX for a large grocery store client, and I had zero idea of how to use any of those technologies, and I had even less knowledge about payrolls. I felt lost, overwelmed and the most stupid guy in the room when I was the cockiest, proudest student in the class because of my *outstanding* grades.

But even with my ego shattered on the floor, I was happy. I was learning like crazy, I was thrilled for every new discovery, I was getting paid, and I could say *Sorry, I got to go to work ;)*.

And I don't blame the University, they prepared me for other things like structured thinking with dozens of math courses. And in a world with hundreds of technologies in every possible software domain, it becomes impossible to prepare a person for any combination of them.

**The University gives you the confidence the real world will destroy in the first week of working**

After some months I got assigned to another project, a Knowledge Transfer from a company which was leaving a custom made program for a clothing factory. This time it was PowerBuilder along with SQL Server. I was familiar with PowerBuilder from the University, and I loved it! SQL Server had SQL (lol) and I knew some of it already because of the Informix project, but I still had to learn the features Informix didn't have. 

So, here we are again, starting from zero in an unkown territory. Less lost, I had some sort of experience, still doing silly junior's questions and breaking stuff now and then (I'll never forget my first **DELETE FROM TABLE**, and I say "first" because I did a few).

And that's the thing I love of working in software companies, every few months a new project arrives: new faces, new industry, new use cases, new learnings. 

**Feeling a little lost, but less, everytime.**

We hire juniors for simple, repetivive, boring tasks like building unit tests for reducing the technical debt. Or to be on-call for supporting an application. Or for documenting purposes. None of those tasks will break the system while they learn and build up confidence, in themselves and with the team.

But AI is good for repetitive tasks, right? 

**Even if AI does the repetitive stuff, someone has to point it in the right direction and trigger it.**

Now, there are two rules for hiring a junior:

1. He must be good at something: it could be in backend, or frontend, or database. Anything, but good for the project's most basic needs.

2. He must be cheap: an expensive junior will eat the project's margin.

Are those things going to change with AI? The answer is **no**.

And I'd like to add a new one:

3. **He must be good using AI**

A junior is starting to fill the boxes, depicted as dots. Some juniors are initially assigned to projects as testing automators, some others as on-call, or as unit tester fillers to meet the project's quality benchmark. And obviously the depth is superficial while the team builds trust on him before giving higher responsibilities.


**Mentoring a junior developer is like nurturing a plant. It takes time and patience.**

But most companies or projects don't have patience, budget or sufficient repetitive tasks. Or that's what they think. As I said before, the *The lack of specs will become the technical debt of the future* and I am no saying by any means that a junior will be responsible of creating new specs or critical ones. But there are specs that are indeed simple and repetitive like CRUD specs that don't exist today, and given the spec debt there could be dozens of missing specs in a single project. Sounds like a job a junior, right?

Now, this is only worthy if the application is still functional and not in plan to be decomissioned. Otherwise is like today's effort to keep an application documented for no purpose.

**Every technology effort must bring a business value. Otherwise is just sport**

The junior must add value to a team instead of babysitting him, as any member of a team he is an investment and the investment must be fruitful. So, how to prepare? How to earn the opportunity to demonstrate you can add value to a team of seniors?

Here are some recommendations to my junior self, things that I would change if I'd start over:

1. **Don't waste time**, every day without learning something new is a wasted day. 

2. **Invest time in networking with others**, late in the career I understood that relationships took me further than grade notes.

3. **Stay humble, stay hungry**, no matter how good you are there will be always someone better (or something like AI). So keep learning and keep sharing your knowledge with others.

4. **Don't stay put**, the technology ocean is bast and deep, learn how to sail those waters. Don't stay too long on the shore, don't dive too deep. 


The opportunities are out there but now companies expect more from the people now that AI falicitates learning. So, it's expected that you can learn faster. Proctored learning is not longer an obstacle. 



## The Senior

Recently I reencountered with an University colleage after two decades. She joined the largest utilities company in the city when she left the University, that was the dream job back in the students because of the benefits and stability. I remember being jealous of the class mates that got a job in that company. However, she told me she felt stuck for years already, the politic game to get promoted was not fair and no matter what technology you master, you cannot decide, you cannot experiment, you cannot feel the overwellming sensation of being lost ever again.






In a world where anyone can speak their own native language, then anyone should be able to create any program, right?

Or at least that's what the AI tech companies keep telling us. So, if that were true, then the Software Engineering career wouldn't be necessary either, right? Only for those blue collar technicians who'd maintain the machines running.

But speaking English doesn't make you a great writer, or a philosopher or an engineer. The natural language is what we use to communicate with others but words don't build the world, actions do. An LLM model will create an application for sure, and it will test it, and even deploy it, but as easy as it sound is as easy as it will fail because we have built dozens of controls and steps to make sure the final user, and the business, never gets disrupted or frustrated.

But Juniors don't know these controls by heart: feature flags, blue/green deployments, canary deployments, feature branches, PRs, linterns, among others.

**Well, neither business users**
